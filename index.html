Credit Card Rewards Calculator: Find the Best Credit Card for Your Spending

Walk into any conversation about credit cards and someone will tell you their card is "the best one." What they usually mean is that it is the best one for them — for their restaurants, their grocery runs, their flights. A credit card rewards calculator exists to answer the harder, personal question: which card puts the most money back in your pocket, given the way you actually spend? PerkWise does exactly that. You enter your monthly spend across six everyday categories, and it computes the real annual value of every card in your wallet — rewards earned minus the annual fee — then ranks them, checks whether each fee is worth paying, and even finds the two-card combination that squeezes the most out of your budget.

This matters more than most people realize. Industry surveys consistently show that a large share of rewards-earning cardholders never do the math: they pick a card for its headline rate ("5% back on dining!"), pay a $95 or $150 annual fee, and quietly earn less than they would have with a free 2% flat card. The headline rate is marketing. The net value — what lands in your pocket after the fee — is the truth. A good calculator closes that gap in about sixty seconds.

How credit card earn rates actually work

Every rewards card is really a small table of earn rates, one per spending category. A typical card might pay 5% on dining, 3% on groceries, 2% on travel and 1% on everything else. The "everything else" rate is called the flat or base rate, and it is secretly the most important number on the card, because for most households the majority of monthly spend falls outside bonus categories. A card advertising 5% on dining but 1% on everything else can easily lose to a boring 2%-on-everything card if you only eat out twice a month.

Two more mechanics shape the real payout. First, bonus caps: many cards pay the elevated rate only up to a yearly spend limit — say, 5% on groceries up to $6,000 per year — and drop to the flat rate beyond it. If your family spends $900 a month on groceries, you blow through that cap by July, and the "6% grocery card" behaves like a 1% card for the rest of the year. PerkWise models caps, which is why its numbers stay honest for heavy spenders. Second, the annual fee: a $95 fee means the card starts every year $95 in the hole. It must earn $95 more than your best free alternative just to break even — a hurdle many fee cards never clear for moderate spenders.

The math itself is simple multiplication: monthly spend × 12 × earn rate, summed across categories, minus the fee. What makes it feel complicated is doing it across five cards and six categories by hand. That is precisely what a calculator automates — and why doing it once, with your real numbers, often changes which plastic you reach for.

Finding the best credit card for your spending

Search "best credit card" and you will get a listicle. The problem with listicles is that they rank cards for a hypothetical average person. You are not hypothetical. The best credit card for your spending is a function of three personal inputs: how much you spend, where you spend it, and what you already hold. Change any one and the winner changes.

Consider two households. Household A spends $400 a month on dining, $300 on groceries, and $200 on travel. Household B spends $100 on dining, $700 on groceries, and $50 on travel. A 5%-on-dining card with no fee is a star for Household A and nearly pointless for Household B, which does far better with a high grocery rate even if that card charges $150 a year. Neither household is "right" — they simply have different spend fingerprints, and the winning card follows the fingerprint, not the marketing.

This is the workflow PerkWise is built around. First, set your monthly spend per category with the sliders — be honest, using a recent bank or card statement if you can. Second, build your wallet: start from the included card archetypes (a flat 2% card, a 5% dining card, a $95 travel card, a $150 grocery card, and a premium $550 card) and edit the rates to match cards you actually own or are considering. Every card is editable because real cards change their rates and your situation changes. Third, read the ranking. The cards are ordered by net annual value — rewards minus fee — computed on your numbers. If the winner surprises you, that surprise is the entire point of the exercise.

A practical tip: run the calculator twice — once with your current cards, and once with a card you are thinking of applying for. The difference in net value is the card's true yearly benefit to you, which you can weigh against any signup bonus. A $200 signup bonus on a card that nets you $40 less per year than your current setup is a bad deal by year six. People rarely do this subtraction; now you can do it in seconds.

Is a credit card annual fee worth it? The breakeven math

"Is the annual fee worth it?" is one of the most searched questions in personal finance, and it has a precise mathematical answer: a fee is worth paying when the card's net value exceeds your best no-fee alternative. PerkWise answers it two ways. First, the ranking already subtracts every fee, so if a $95 card sits above your free 2% card, the fee is currently worth it for you. Second, the dedicated fee check tells you the breakeven spend — the total monthly spend at which the fee card starts beating the free baseline, assuming your category mix stays the same.

Why does breakeven matter? Because fees are fixed but rewards scale with spend. A $95 fee card that earns an extra 3% on dining needs about $264 of monthly dining spend to cover its fee versus a 2% flat card ($95 ÷ 12 ÷ 0.03 ≈ $264). Spend less than that on dining and the fee is pure loss; spend more and every extra dollar is profit. Breakeven turns a vague feeling ("$95 seems like a lot") into a concrete number you can compare with your actual budget. PerkWise phrases the verdict plainly: either the card already pays for itself at your spend, it pays for itself once you reach a specific monthly total, or it never catches the free card no matter how much you spend.

There is an important nuance the breakeven number captures: it is always relative to a baseline. A $95 card might beat a free 1.5% card but lose to a free 2% card. That is why PerkWise defaults the baseline to your best no-fee card rather than some imaginary zero — the honest comparison is fee card versus the best free thing you could hold instead. If you are choosing between two fee cards, set the cheaper one as the baseline and read the verdict the same way.

One card or two? The case for a simple 2-card stack

Ask enthusiasts how many cards to carry and you will hear numbers like seven or twelve, each deployed like a surgical instrument. For most people, that is a part-time job nobody applied for. But the jump from one card to two is where the biggest marginal gain lives. The reason is structural: no single card wins every category. A card with a great dining rate usually has a mediocre flat rate, and a great flat-rate card has no bonuses at all. Pair them — bonuses on one, everything else on the other — and you capture most of the available rewards with almost no mental overhead.

PerkWise's combo optimizer finds that pair for you without the spreadsheet. It tests every two-card combination in your wallet, assigns each spending category to whichever of the two pays more on it, subtracts both annual fees, and reports the winning stack plus exactly which card to use where. It also tells you how much the combo beats your best single card per year, so you can decide whether the extra complexity is worth, say, $180 a year. For many households the answer is yes — two cards, two rules ("dining and groceries on card A, everything else on card B"), and the third, fourth and fifth cards add little.

A word of caution the optimizer will not give you: only add a second card if you will actually use it as assigned. Rewards left on the table because you forgot which card to tap are worse than a simpler setup you follow consistently. The best stack is the best one you will execute.

Cashback vs. points: a quick note

PerkWise estimates everything in cashback dollars, which keeps every card comparable. Points and miles can be worth more — or less — than a cent each depending on how you redeem them, and transfer partners add another layer of maybe. If you are a disciplined points optimizer who reliably extracts high value from travel redemptions, treat PerkWise's dollar figures as a conservative floor: your real value may be higher on points-heavy cards. If you mostly redeem points for statement credit or gift cards, the cashback estimate is essentially exact. When in doubt, the cashback number is the honest, comparable baseline.

Honest limitations

PerkWise is an estimation tool, not a financial advisor. It assumes your spending mix stays roughly constant through the year, values all rewards at cash-back rates, and does not model signup bonuses, rotating quarterly categories, foreign transaction fees, or the opportunity cost of carrying a balance — and carrying a balance wipes out rewards many times over through interest, so always pay in full. The starter cards are generic archetypes with descriptive names, not specific bank products; always verify a real card's current rates, caps and fees with the issuer before applying, because issuers change terms. Used with those caveats, a rewards calculator is one of the highest-value five minutes in personal finance: it replaces marketing with arithmetic, and arithmetic does not care about slogans.

Frequently asked questions

How does a credit card rewards calculator work?＋

You enter your monthly spend per category, and the calculator multiplies each category by the card's earn rate, adds everything up over 12 months, and subtracts the annual fee. The result is the card's net annual value in dollars. PerkWise repeats that math for every card in your wallet so you can compare them on one honest number instead of headline rates.

What is the best credit card for my spending?＋

The one with the highest net value on your spend mix — rewards earned minus the annual fee. A 5% dining card wins for heavy restaurant spenders; a free flat 2% card often wins for everyone else. Set your real monthly spend in PerkWise and check the ranking: the winner for your budget may surprise you.

Is a credit card annual fee worth it?＋

A fee is worth paying only if the card earns at least that much more than your best free alternative. PerkWise computes the breakeven spend — the monthly total at which the fee card pulls ahead — and tells you plainly whether it already pays for itself at your spend, pays off at a specific monthly amount, or never beats the free card.

Should I use one credit card or several?＋

For most people, two cards hit the sweet spot: one for bonus categories and one flat-rate card for everything else. No single card wins every category, so a simple 2-card stack captures most available rewards with minimal effort. PerkWise's combo optimizer finds your best pair and shows exactly which card to use for each category.

Do credit card rewards expire?＋

It depends on the program: straight cashback usually does not expire as long as the account is open, while some points and miles programs have expiration policies or forfeit rewards if you close the card. PerkWise estimates earn value only — always check the issuer's terms for expiration and redemption rules before choosing a card.

How accurate are PerkWise's estimates?＋

They are exact arithmetic on the numbers you enter: spend × earn rate × 12, minus fees, with bonus caps modeled. Accuracy depends on your inputs — use real statement numbers for best results. PerkWise values rewards as cashback and ignores signup bonuses, rotating categories, and interest charges, so treat it as a comparison tool, not financial advice.
